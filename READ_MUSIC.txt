Intervals,Major Scale, Sharps, Flats and Enharmonics
An interval is the musical difference between two notes
The smallest interval is a half-step 
You can think of it as the distance between one key on the piano and the very next one regardless of the 
color of either key.  
The next interval is a whole step  - the distance between two keys skipping the one (and only one) in 
between (color of any of the keys doesn't matter )
The white keys on the piano are named for the 7 letters.  The 2 white keys that are next to each other 
and to the left of the set with the two black keys are B and C.  The 2 white keys that are next to each 
other and between the set of keys with the 2 black keys on their left and the 3 black keys on their right.
The white key between the two black keys is D.  The white keys between the 3 black keys are G on the 
left and A on the right.
The black keys are named after the white keys they are next to depending on the scale that is 'in play' 
(more on this later) at the time.  The leftmost black key of the set of two is C# (C sharp) or Db (D flat).  
The rightmost black key of the set of two is D# (D sharp) or Eb (E flat).  The other 3, left to right are 
F#/Gb, G#/Ab and A#/Bb.

All of the natural notes (neither sharp nor flat) are 1 whole step (2 half steps) apart 
EXCEPT;
 B and C  which are 1 half step apart (note they are not separated by a black key)
E and F   also 1 half step apart  (note they are not separated by a black key)
Any note and its sharp (or any note and its flat) are 1 half step apart


D and D# are 1 half step apart as are D and Db
A and A# are 1 half step apart as are A and Ab
2 notes can have the same name - they are called Enharmonic notes
C# and Db,  D# and Eb, E# and F,(E and Fb) F# and Gb, G# and Ab, A# and Bb, B# and C (B and Cb)
You would rarely speak of Cb or Fb or B# or E# 

The C major scale is made up of only the white keys on the piano.  There are no sharps and no flats.
Starting with the C (the white key to the left of the 2 black keys that are together) you will see that the 
first 3 notes have a black key (which is not used in the scale) between them.  This tells us there is a 
whole step between the first two white keys (C and D) since there is a black key (which is a half step 
from the white keys) and a whole step between the next two white keys (D and E) for the same reason.
The next two white keys are right next to each other (E and F) - they are a half step apart. 
The F and G , G and A and A and B are all separated by a black key so they are whole steps apart - that is 
3 whole steps in a row.  The final two notes in the scale (B and C) are next to each other so we end on a 
half step.  You can see that this shows us how the major scale is defined.  You can always use that as a 
reference if you forget.
The Major Scale is defined as;
A Whole Step
A Whole Step
A Half Step
A Whole Step
A Whole Step
A Whole Step
A Half Step
Or (Whole, Whole, Half, Whole, Whole, Whole, Half)
It can be described as two sequences of (Whole, Whole, Half) separated by a Whole step.  You don't 
need to remember this at this time but the sequence of (Whole, Whole Half) is called a tetrachord.
The first note of each scale is called the 'root note' or 'the 1'.
The second note of each scale is called the 'second' or 'the 2'.
The third note of each scale is called 'the third' or '3', the fourth note is 'the fourth' or '4', the fifth is 
'the fifth' or '5', the sixth is 'the sixth' or '6'.
It goes that way for all of the notes up to the seventh or 'the 7' ending with the octave which is 'the 
root' again.
The 'second', the 'third', the 'fourth' etc can also refer to the distance between that note and the 'first' 
or root note.
Using the C major scale for an example:
An interval of 'a second' is always a whole step (e.g. C and D)
An interval of 'a third' is always two whole steps (e.g. C and E)
An interval of 'a fourth' is always two whole steps and a half step (C and F) (this is a tetrachord - tetra 
means 4)
An interval of 'a fifth' is always two whole steps, a half step and another whole step (C and G) 
It can also be referred to as 3 whole steps and a half step.
An interval of 'a sixth' is always 4 whole steps and a half step (C and A)
An interval of 'a seventh' is always 5 whole steps and a half step (A and B)
Using the G major scale we have 
The second (G and A)
The third (G and B)
The fourth (G and C)
The fifth (G and D)
The sixth (G and E)
The seventh (G and F#)





The Major Scale always starts (and ends) on the note named by the Key Signature. This is called the Root 
Note or just 'the root'
The C Major Scale would then start on the C Note
C D E F G A B C
The G Major Scale would start on the G Note.
G A B C D E F# G
 
Key Signatures
Memorize this sequence 'B E A D G C F' (backwards and forwards) 
(Remember the word Bead and then GCF but then you have to remember to move the F)

F B E A D G C (flats) [we will use this later for the circle of fourths]
C G D A E B F (sharps) [we will use this later for the circle of fifths]

Flats;
Key of F - one flat (Bb) - B is the next letter after F)
Key of Bb - two flats (Bb and Eb) E is the next letter after B
Key of Eb - three flats (Bb, Eb and Ab) A is the next letter after E
Key of Ab - four flats (Bb, Eb, Ab and Db) D is the next letter after A
Key of Db - five flats (Bb, Eb, Ab, Db and Gb) G is the next letter after D
Each subsequent key signature has the same flatted notes as the previous key signature plus 1 more
And they are always added in the same order

This last one is almost never seen and can safely be ignored
Key of Gb - six flats (Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb and Cb/B) C is the next letter after G

You can always know which new note to make sharp - the one before (alphabetically) the one the
Key signature is named after;  e.g. G ==> F# , D ==> C#, A==>G#, etc...always in the same order
Key of C - No Sharps or Flats
Key of G - 1 sharp (F#)  
Key of D - 2 sharps (F# and C#)
Key of A - 3 sharps (F#, C#, G#)
Key of E - 4 sharps (F#, C#, G# and D#)
Key of B - 5 sharps (F#, C#, G#, D# and A#)
These last two are rare and can be safely ignored for now  They are shown here for completeness.
Key of F# - 6 sharps (F#, C#, G#, D#, A# and E#/F)
Key of C# - 7 sharps (F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, E#/F and B#/C)

They alternate between the 'sets of black keys' the first of the set of 3 then the first of the set of 2
Then the 2nd of the set of 3 then the second of the set of 2 then the 3rd of the set of 3
C has no sharps
G has one - the leftmost of the set of three black keys
D has two - the one from G and the leftmost of the set of two black keys
A has three - the two from D and the middle one from the set of three
E has four - the three from A and the rightmost one from the set of two
B has five - the four from above and the rightmost one from the set of three


Most adjacent guitar strings are tuned a perfect fourth apart.
The B string is different — it is tuned a major third apart, which is one half step smaller.
You can think of the B string as starting one fret “behind,” so patterns crossing the B string usually need to move one fret higher to compensate.

…except for the B string.

That is actually excellent recurring pedagogy.

Because the B string exception is one of the biggest stumbling blocks for guitarists learning:

intervals
scale shapes
triads
chord inversions
sightreading on guitar
movable patterns

…and beginners constantly forget it.

Turning it into a running phrase:

“…except for the B string.”

does several useful things simultaneously:

reinforces the tuning irregularity
reduces frustration
creates anticipation/humor
gives students a mental checkpoint
normalizes the adjustment instead of making it feel like failure

And because it recurs repeatedly, it becomes a kind of cognitive anchor.

Honestly, experienced guitarists almost internally say this phrase already.

Your version simply makes it explicit and memorable.

You could even formally introduce it once:

The guitar is tuned mostly in fourths…
…except for the B string.

Then later:

“Move this shape up one string… except for the B string.”
“The interval pattern repeats… except for the B string.”
“The inversion shape is identical… except for the B string.”
“The scale pattern shifts… except for the B string.”

That becomes both humorous and educational.

And it subtly teaches an important truth:
the fretboard is highly logical, but not perfectly symmetrical.

That is one of the biggest conceptual hurdles for guitarists.

I actually think touches like this may become part of the personality of the guide:

practical
slightly humorous
pattern-focused
conversational
non-intimidating

That tone fits your intended audience extremely well.
