Bare Essentials Music Theory
A Practical Guide for Guitarists, Adult Learners, and Beginning Musicians
Working Goals of This Guide

This guide is not intended to be a comprehensive conservatory-level theory textbook. Its purpose is to provide the minimum practical music theory necessary to:

Understand the layout of the guitar fretboard
Develop usable relative pitch
Learn basic chord construction and harmony
Understand rhythm and counting
Begin basic sightreading
Recognize musical patterns instead of memorizing isolated facts
Support improvisation and chord-tone soloing

The emphasis throughout this guide is on relationships, patterns, and practical musicianship.

Music theory is not a collection of arbitrary rules. It is a system of recognizable relationships between sounds, intervals, chords, rhythms, and visual patterns.

A recurring theme of this guide is that the same musical concepts appear repeatedly in different forms:

on the fretboard
on the staff
on the piano keyboard
in chord structures
in scales
in what we hear

Understanding those relationships is far more valuable than memorizing disconnected information.



Introduction: Relative Pitch vs. Perfect Pitch

Many beginning musicians believe that successful musicians are born with extraordinary natural abilities such as perfect pitch.

Perfect pitch — the ability to identify notes without a reference tone — is real, but it is rare and completely unnecessary for becoming a capable musician.

Most musicians rely primarily on relative pitch:

recognizing distances between notes
hearing tension and resolution
identifying chord quality
hearing melodic movement
recognizing rhythmic placement

Relative pitch is trainable.

In fact, every time you:

practice scales
compare major and minor chords
play intervals
hear a chord progression
sing notes while playing
recognize a melody

…you are already training your ear.

Ear training is not a separate magical discipline. It naturally develops alongside:

rhythm practice
chord study
sightreading
scale practice
improvisation

The goal is not to become a human tuning fork.

The goal is to recognize musical relationships.

Section 1 — Notes, Pitch, and the Musical Alphabet
The Musical Alphabet

Music in Western theory uses the note names:

A B C D E F G

After G, the pattern repeats.

These notes repeat in higher and lower octaves.

A piano keyboard is one of the easiest ways to visualize musical relationships, even if you never intend to play piano.

(Include one-octave and two-octave keyboard diagrams.)

The repeating pattern of black and white keys demonstrates:

note repetition
octaves
half steps
whole steps
enharmonic notes
Half Steps and Whole Steps

A half step is the smallest distance commonly used in Western music.

Examples:

E to F
B to C
C to C#

A whole step equals two half steps.

Examples:

C to D
F to G
Sharps, Flats, and Enharmonics

A sharp (#) raises a note by one half step. A flat (b) lowers a note by one half step.

Examples:

C# and Db are enharmonic equivalents
F# and Gb are enharmonic equivalents

Although these notes sound the same on modern instruments, the spelling matters in music theory because note names help reveal harmonic structure.

An A is always some flavor of A. It may be:

A natural
A sharp
A flat
A double sharp
A double flat

…but it is still functioning as a form of A.

That idea becomes extremely important later when reading chords and notation.

Section 2 — Intervals and Scale Construction
What Is an Interval?

An interval describes the distance between two notes.

Intervals can describe:

the position of a note relative to a scale
or the distance between any two notes

This distinction is important.

For example:

the fourth note of a scale is called the 4th scale degree
the distance between two notes may also form an interval of a fourth
Basic Interval Naming

Intervals are named by counting letter names.

Examples:

C to D = second
C to E = third
C to F = fourth
C to G = fifth

Some interval families are described as:

major/minor
perfect
augmented/diminished
Major and Minor Intervals
2nds
3rds
6ths
7ths

may be major or minor.

Perfect Intervals
4ths
5ths
octaves

are called perfect intervals.

Augmented and Diminished

Any interval may be enlarged or reduced:

augmented = raised
diminished = lowered

The guide will focus primarily on the intervals most useful for beginning harmony and guitar study.

Why Intervals Matter

Intervals are one of the most important ideas in music.

They define:

scales
chords
melodies
harmony
fretboard shapes
ear training

A musician who understands intervals can often recognize musical structures before knowing every individual note.

Section 3 — Major and Minor Scales
The Chromatic Scale

The chromatic scale contains all 12 notes used in Western music.

Example:

C C# D D# E F F# G G# A A# B

(or enharmonic equivalents)

Major Scale Structure

The major scale follows the pattern:

Whole Whole Half Whole Whole Whole Half

Natural Minor Scale Structure

The natural minor scale follows the pattern:

Whole Half Whole Whole Half Whole Whole

The guide may briefly mention harmonic and melodic minor scales for completeness, but they are not essential for the early goals of this course.

Scale Degrees

The notes of a scale are often identified by number:

1 2 3 4 5 6 7

These numbers become extremely important when discussing:

intervals
chord construction
harmony
improvisation
chord-tone soloing
Section 4 — Chords and Harmony
Building Chords by Stacking Thirds

Most common chords are built by stacking thirds.

Example:

C E G

The notes skip every other letter.

This “stacked thirds” structure is one of the most important visual and harmonic patterns in music.

On written notation:

line-line-line
or space-space-space

usually indicates stacked thirds.

This allows musicians to recognize chord structures quickly without reading every individual note separately.

Major and Minor Triads

A triad contains three distinct notes:

root
third
fifth
Major Triad
root
major third
perfect fifth
Minor Triad
root
minor third
perfect fifth

The third determines whether the chord sounds major or minor.

Seventh Chords

Adding another stacked third creates a seventh chord.

Examples:

major seventh
dominant seventh
minor seventh

The guide will introduce basic seventh chords but will not focus heavily on extended harmony.

Voicings

A voicing is a particular arrangement of chord tones.

Example:

An E major chord on guitar may contain:

E B E G# B E

Even though notes repeat, the chord is still considered E major because the essential chord tones are:

E G# B

Inversions

An inversion occurs when a chord tone other than the root becomes the lowest note.

Understanding inversions is essential for:

guitar triad shapes
smooth voice leading
fretboard navigation
chord recognition
Section 5 — Keys, Diatonic Harmony, and the Circle of Fifths
The Key Concept

A key establishes a tonal center.

The chords and notes of a key are called diatonic to that key.

Diatonic Harmony

Building triads on each scale degree creates the diatonic chords of a key.

In the key of C major:

C major
D minor
E minor
F major
G major
A minor
B diminished

The guide will introduce these relationships primarily to support:

chord progressions
guitar harmony
chord-tone soloing
sightreading recognition
Key Signatures

A key signature indicates which notes are consistently sharp or flat throughout a piece.

This allows music to be written more clearly without repeating accidentals constantly.

The Circle of Fifths

The Circle of Fifths organizes keys by relationships.

Moving clockwise adds sharps. Moving counterclockwise adds flats.

Order of Sharps

F# C# G# D# A# E# B#

Order of Flats

Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb Fb

Each new key keeps the previous sharps or flats and adds one more.

The circle also reveals relationships between:

keys
chord functions
transposition
guitar patterns

Section 6 — Rhythm and Time
Why Rhythm Matters

Pitch without rhythm is incomplete music.

A musician with excellent rhythm can often sound more convincing than one with advanced harmonic knowledge but poor timing.

Measures and Time Signatures

Introduce:

measures
bar lines
3/4 time
4/4 time

Mention that many other time signatures exist.

Note Values

Introduce:

whole notes
half notes
quarter notes
eighth notes
sixteenth notes
thirty-second notes

Include rests for each.

Beams and Ties

Students should recognize:

beams connecting notes
ties joining note durations

because these appear frequently in sightreading.

Counting and Internal Pulse

Students should:

count rhythms aloud
tap rhythms
clap subdivisions
practice maintaining steady pulse

Rhythm is one of the most trainable musical skills.

Section 7 — Guitar Fretboard Essentials
The Chromatic Nature of the Fretboard

Each fret represents one half step.

The pattern repeats across the neck.

Relationships Between Strings

Most adjacent strings are tuned in fourths.

The exception is between the G and B strings, which are tuned a major third apart.

Understanding this relationship explains:

chord shapes
scale patterns
triad inversions
movable forms
Landmark Relationships

The:

5th fret of one string
equals the open note of the next higher string

The:

7th fret of one string
equals the open note of the next lower string

These relationships greatly simplify fretboard navigation.

Learning the Notes of the Fretboard

Students should eventually know the note names of the first 12 frets on every string.

This is far more manageable than many beginners initially assume.

Intervals on a Single String

Examples:

3rd fret = minor third
5th fret = perfect fourth
7th fret = perfect fifth
12th fret = octave

Understanding intervals geometrically helps unify:

scales
chords
improvisation
ear training
Triads Across String Sets

Students should learn:

major triads
minor triads
inversions

across all four adjacent three-string sets.

This develops:

fretboard fluency
chord recognition
voice leading
improvisational freedom
Open and Movable Chord Shapes

Introduce:

C A G E D open forms
several movable forms

Students should identify:

chord tones
voicings
location of the third

and understand how changing the third alters chord quality.

Three-Note-Per-String Scale Shapes

Introduce:

major patterns
natural minor patterns

starting from:

6th string roots
5th string roots
Chord-Tone Soloing

Introduce the basic idea that melodies and solos can emphasize chord tones during chord changes.

The goal is not advanced jazz improvisation.

The goal is to begin hearing harmony actively while playing.

Section 8 — Introductory Sightreading
Clefs and Staves

Introduce:

treble clef
bass clef
staves
ledger lines
key signatures
Treble Clef Landmarks

The treble clef wraps around the G line.

The spaces spell:

F A C E

This is not merely a mnemonic.

It also forms recognizable chord structures.

The middle three treble-clef lines form:

G B D

which outlines a G major chord.

Bass Clef Landmarks

The bass clef dots surround the F line.

The bass-clef spaces form:

A C E G

Again, these are harmonic structures, not merely memory tricks.

The middle bass-clef lines form:

B D F

Ledger Line Continuity

The line-space-line-space pattern continues beyond the staff.

Understanding this continuity simplifies reading ledger lines dramatically.

Recognizing Chord Shapes Visually

One important goal of sightreading is recognizing patterns instead of decoding every note individually.

A guitarist should eventually be able to recognize:

stacked thirds
triads
chord shapes
inversions

visually and connect them directly to:

sounds
fretboard positions
harmonic function
Connecting the Staff to the Instrument

Students should see how a written note corresponds to:

piano keys
fretboard locations
interval relationships

This integration is one of the major goals of this guide.

Closing Thoughts

Music theory is often presented as a collection of rules to memorize.

In reality, it is a system of recurring patterns and relationships.

The same structures appear repeatedly:

on the staff
on the fretboard
on the keyboard
in chords
in scales
in melodies
in rhythm
in what we hear

A practical musician does not need to memorize everything at once.

The goal is gradual familiarity.

Over time:

patterns become recognizable
sounds become predictable
the fretboard becomes navigable
notation becomes meaningful
harmony becomes understandable

And eventually, music begins to feel less like decoding information and more like understanding a language.
